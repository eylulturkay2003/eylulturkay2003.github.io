BSC LITERATURE DISSERTATION

Pancreatic cancer metabolism

A literature-based BSc dissertation examining metabolic plasticity in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and the rationale for targeting glycolytic and mitochondrial energy pathways.

For viewing only — reproduction or redistribution is not permitted.

PROJECT OVERVIEW

Metabolic flexibility may create vulnerabilities—and routes around treatment.

PDAC develops in a nutrient- and oxygen-constrained tumour environment. Published research discussed in the dissertation describes tumour cells adapting how they acquire and use energy. This flexibility is important when evaluating any proposed metabolic treatment: inhibiting one route may leave alternative routes available.

RESEARCH QUESTION

Could paired metabolic targets constrain compensatory adaptation?

The review considers how glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) contribute to PDAC metabolism, and whether targeting both could address pathway compensation more effectively than a single target. It is a synthesis question, not a clinical treatment test.

BACKGROUND: GLYCOLYSIS & OXPHOS

Energy pathways are interconnected, not isolated switches.

Glycolysis converts glucose to metabolic intermediates and can support ATP production and biosynthesis. Mitochondrial OXPHOS uses electron transport and a proton gradient to generate ATP and support other cellular processes. The balance between these routes can change with nutrient availability, oxygen conditions, oncogenic signalling and treatment pressure.

Consequently, describing PDAC as exclusively “glycolytic” or “oxidative” can oversimplify heterogeneous tumours and cell states. The review's central biological theme is metabolic plasticity: cells may shift pathway use as conditions change.

COMPLEX I & NDUFS1

A proposed mitochondrial vulnerability in the cited literature.

The dissertation discusses mitochondrial Complex I and NDUFS1, a Complex I subunit, as a possible point of intervention in the published work it reviews. CRISPR-Cas9-mediated disruption is discussed as an experimental strategy in that literature. I did not perform CRISPR experiments for this literature-based dissertation.

Complex I targeting is considered in relation to OXPHOS dependence and possible effects on PDAC cell viability or adaptation. Such a rationale does not by itself show that NDUFS1 is a safe, selective or clinically effective target.

MAIN ARGUMENT: COMBINATION RATIONALE

Dual targeting is a proposal to test, not a demonstrated therapy.

If inhibition of glycolysis encourages greater reliance on mitochondrial respiration, and mitochondrial inhibition encourages compensatory use of glycolytic metabolism, then inhibiting both pathways could in principle narrow those escape routes. This is a mechanistic rationale for investigation, not a result established by the dissertation author's own experiment.

The review frames this strategy against therapeutic resistance: adaptive pathway shifts and tumour heterogeneity may enable persistence under treatment. Whether combined metabolic inhibition can overcome those processes depends on tumour context, target engagement, dose, toxicity and the ability of cells to use other substrates or pathways.

APPROACH & MY CONTRIBUTION

Critical synthesis of published evidence.

This is a literature-based dissertation. My contribution was to review and synthesize published evidence about PDAC metabolism, glycolysis, OXPHOS and the proposed Complex I/NDUFS1 strategy. No personal cell-culture, CRISPR, animal or clinical experiments are reported as part of this work.

The dissertation discusses experimental studies as secondary evidence. Their assays, models and findings belong to the cited research teams; this web summary does not reassign those data to me.

FINDINGS & INTERPRETATION

Metabolic adaptation complicates single-target intervention.

The review's main argument is that PDAC metabolic behaviour is context-dependent and that pathway redundancy or adaptation may limit single-pathway strategies. Dual inhibition of glycolysis and OXPHOS is presented as a proposed way to test whether compensation can be constrained. It was not demonstrated as an effective treatment by my dissertation.

LIMITATIONS & VALIDATION REQUIRED

Evidence synthesis cannot establish therapeutic benefit.

  • The dissertation is literature-based and does not supply original experimental validation.
  • Conclusions depend on the models, assays and study designs used in the cited literature.
  • Metabolic states vary across tumours, microenvironments and treatment conditions; a pathway dependency may not generalize.
  • Dual targeting requires testing for target engagement, compensatory pathway use, selectivity and toxicity.
  • Preclinical findings would need independent replication, appropriate in-vivo studies and clinical evidence before supporting patient treatment.

No claim is made that a stated number of papers was screened systematically; a reproducible systematic-search protocol was not documented in the source as reviewed for this summary.

SKILLS DEMONSTRATED

Integrating tumour metabolism with therapeutic reasoning.

  • Literature search, selection and synthesis.
  • Explaining glycolysis and mitochondrial OXPHOS in cancer context.
  • Relating Complex I/NDUFS1 to proposed metabolic intervention.
  • Evaluating pathway compensation, heterogeneity and resistance.
  • Distinguishing a research hypothesis from experimentally or clinically demonstrated efficacy.
