ACADEMIC PRESENTATION · LITERATURE-BASED

What Have We Learned from Next-Generation Sequencing of Cancer Genomes?

A course presentation tracing the shift from single-gene tests to genome-wide cancer profiling and asking what those data can—and cannot—support clinically.

For viewing only — reproduction or redistribution is not permitted.

OVERVIEW

From individual genes to evolving tumour genomes.

The presentation synthesizes published work on how next-generation sequencing (NGS) detects single-nucleotide variants, copy-number changes and structural variants at genome scale. It frames cancer as heterogeneous and dynamically evolving, rather than explained by a single mutation alone.

APPROACH

How the presentation explains genomic evidence.

It outlines tumour–normal comparison, sequencing and library preparation, read alignment and bioinformatic variant calling. The presentation connects recurrent alterations and clonal/subclonal populations to potential biological and clinical interpretations. It is a literature-based academic presentation, not a sequencing study conducted by me.

STRENGTHS & INSIGHTS

Broader detection can reveal complexity.

  • Genome-wide profiling can identify multiple classes of alterations in one analysis.
  • Clonal and subclonal patterns can inform tumour evolution and possible resistance mechanisms.
  • Matched tumour–normal analysis helps distinguish somatic from inherited variation.
  • NGS can support tumour profiling, hereditary-risk assessment, selected treatment decisions and disease monitoring, depending on the assay and clinical context.

INTERPRETATION CHALLENGES

Detection is shaped by sampling and pipeline choices.

Coverage, tumour purity, sequencing depth, error rates, variant thresholds and bioinformatic pipelines affect what is detected. Bulk sequencing averages across cells and can miss low-frequency subclones; a single timepoint cannot fully capture later evolution. Platform characteristics also influence sensitivity to different alteration types.

CLINICAL ACTIONABILITY

A detected variant is not automatically actionable.

The presentation emphasizes that only a minority of observed variants are clinically actionable. Distinguishing driver from passenger changes and connecting a variant to a validated treatment benefit requires biological evidence and clinical interpretation. Tumour mutational burden, microsatellite instability and circulating tumour DNA have context-dependent predictive or monitoring value; sensitivity may be limited, particularly in low-burden disease.

LIMITATIONS & FUTURE DIRECTIONS

More data do not remove the interpretation bottleneck.

The presentation identifies cost, access, turnaround time, ethical considerations and specialist interpretation as practical constraints. It discusses single-cell sequencing, long-read methods and multi-omics as ways to address selected blind spots, not as solutions that remove validation or clinical-utility requirements.

MY CONTRIBUTION & SKILLS

Literature synthesis and scientific communication.

I prepared and presented the academic synthesis, organizing evidence into methods, biological insight, clinical uses and limitations. Skills demonstrated include critical literature review, genomic terminology, connecting assay outputs to clinical questions, and visual communication.
